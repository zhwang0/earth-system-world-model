The gap

Emulators reproduce the simulator. They offer no mechanism to ask what happens if you change the forest itself.

Process-based Earth-system models are expensive, so the field has learned to emulate them with neural networks. Those emulators are fast and accurate — and inert. Given climate forcing and an initial state they replay a trajectory, but there is no input for logging, wildfire, replanting, or a management policy.

The paper draws the analogy to early video generators: high-fidelity pixel streams with nothing to interact with. What a digital twin needs is the other thing — a model you can intervene on, that answers coherently across every coupled variable.

The obstacle is supervision. Game and robotics world models learn from logged actions; Earth-system simulator runs contain no action labels at all. Nobody recorded "a human thinned this stand in 1997", because nobody did.

The paper's move is to notice that every observed transition already is an action. If canopy height went up 0.4 m this year, treat that as a +0.4 m action and learn the mapping. Label-free supervision, from data that already exists.

Method

Three ideas, one model

01

Transition-action pretraining

Every state change becomes its own action label. For the controlled variable — here canopy height — the action is simply the observed change:

atS = yt+1S − ytS

A positive action is accelerated structural growth; a negative one is a structural reduction such as harvest or disturbance. No annotation required.

02

An action gate

One model, two modes. With no action, a natural-dynamics head predicts the height increment and the model behaves as an ordinary emulator. With an action, the encoded action takes over:

ĥt+1 = ht + Dh(Δĥt)  ·  no action
ĥt+1 = ht + Dh(ât)  ·  action given

The gate is what stops the network from ignoring the action — and from losing the ability to predict when none is given.

03

Masked response learning

When you edit one variable, the others are out of date. So during action-conditioned training the non-height variables at the edited step are masked out, forcing the model to infer the coupled response:

ŷt+1−h = gθ(zt, ĥt+1)

Biomass, soil carbon and the fluxes are reconstructed from the latent state and the new height alone — exactly the situation at inference time.

What exactly is being computed here?

The page downloads the trained weights for your chosen stand age and runs a 40-year autoregressive rollout in a background thread, seeded by the first five years of the ED simulation and driven by that site's real historical climate (1981–2021). An action is a change in canopy height, in metres, applied in a chosen year; the model's gate substitutes it for its own growth prediction and the decoder infers the other six variables from the latent state and the new height.

Every value is a December value. The model reads and predicts the state of each December, which is what the stocks — height, biomass, soil carbon — are naturally measured as. For leaf area and the three carbon fluxes it matters more: those are instantaneous rates, so at a seasonal forest December is midwinter rather than the annual mean. At the eastern North America cells, December gross primary production is around 0.16 kgC m⁻² yr⁻¹ against an annual mean near 1.94. Those four charts are marked Dec.

An action is not a direct edit. The height change the model applies passes through a learned decoder, so asking for −2.0 m produces about −2.05 m. And the model does not recover: after a disturbance it carries the difference forward rather than converging back to the undisturbed trajectory.

Actions are reported against the range the model actually saw in training — ED's own yearly height changes across the 203 study cells. Inside that range you are asking a fair question; outside it you are extrapolating, and the interface says so.

Don't take our word for it: run the self-check. It replays rollouts stored from the original PyTorch model and reports the error your browser actually achieves.

Honest limits

What this model does not do

The paper calls itself an exploratory step, and the demo above is built to show the edges rather than hide them.

It does not recover

After a disturbance the model carries the height difference forward indefinitely. A real stand regrows; this one holds its loss to year 40. Treat trajectories after a large intervention as a shifted baseline, not a recovery curve.

Mature forests barely respond

At 150 and 300 years a ±5% change in yearly growth moves canopy height by well under a metre by year 40, and biomass hardly at all. That is consistent with slow structural change near maturity — and it means the mature ages are the least interesting to poke.

One controlled variable

Only canopy height can be edited. Everything else is inferred. You cannot directly set soil carbon or impose a drought; forcings are the historical record, 1981–2021.

Six regions, not the globe

Training and evaluation used 203 cells across six regions. The globe lets you visit other forests because it is informative to see, but those runs are extrapolation and are labelled as such.

It can disagree with the simulator

At some sites ED's own canopy rises while its biomass falls — climate-driven fire that burns biomass while the tallest trees survive. The emulator does not reproduce that pattern everywhere. Where the ED line and the model line diverge, the chart shows it.

A learned surrogate, not advice

The target solver inverts a neural emulator. It is a way to interrogate what the model has learned about structure and carbon — not a forestry prescription, a yield projection, or a policy instrument.
